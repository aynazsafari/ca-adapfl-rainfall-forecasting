# R1-m1: physical rationale and limits of the six climate indices

Prepared before evaluation-score access. This is evidence for a later revision response, not an edit to the manuscript or a claim that the indices improve accuracy. The reviewer asks why Pacific, Indian Ocean and Atlantic indices were chosen, particularly an AMO predictor at a stated one-month lag (review document P0010; audit item R1-m1).

The defensible rationale is **four complementary, potentially redundant ENSO measurements, one interacting Indian Ocean dipole measurement, and one exploratory Atlantic background covariate**. It is not six independent physical causes. NOAA describes SST and multivariate indices as alternative summaries of a coupled ocean–atmosphere process. [NOAA ENSO information](https://www.psl.noaa.gov/enso/index.html)

The ENSO–monsoon relationship cannot be assumed constant. Krishna Kumar et al. analyze a 140-year record and discuss weakening of the inverse relationship, with possible changes in Walker circulation and Eurasian warming. That supports acknowledging conditional and time-varying relationships, not selecting a universal sign or lag. This audit used the publication's **abstract in the official PubMed record only**, not inaccessible full-text pages. [Krishna Kumar et al. (1999)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10381876/)

The present AMV definition is researcher-derived: calendar-month SST anomalies relative to **1951–1980**, native ERSSTv6 whole-cell WGS84 area weighting over North Atlantic **0–60°N, 80°W–0°** minus global ocean **60°S–60°N**, without temporal smoothing or Kaplan splicing. It is not an official NOAA AMO series or an exact reproduction of either Atlantic paper. Trenberth and Shea use annual anomalies relative to 1901–1970 and a centered low-pass filter; copying that filter into forecast features would require future values. [Trenberth and Shea (2006)](https://staff.cgd.ucar.edu/trenbert/trenberth.pdf/TrenberthSheaHurricanes2006GRL026894.pdf), p.2, Figs.1–3. Given the different definition and 44-year study span, **nonstationarity and causal attribution remain unverified**; this is an inference about the limits of our evidence, not a finding of these papers about this reconstruction.

The actual frozen calendar must replace the ambiguous old “one-month AMO lag.” For target month `t`, issue time is its first day at 00:00 UTC; origin is `t−1`. Twelve historical origin rows run from `t−12` through `t−1`. Each climate period ends one month before its row origin, so the index sequence ends **`t−13` through `t−2`**; the attention context uses its latest eligible row, **`t−2`**. The oldest underlying support for the overlapping ONI and MEIv2 measures is `t−15` and `t−14`, respectively. Meteorological/supplement support is `t−15` through `t−4`. These are conservative **archive-availability assumptions**, not source-derived propagation lags or empirical lag optima. They are checked against packaged `caadapfl/features.py`, lines 53–99 and 104–129. ERSSTv6 postdates evaluation, and current revised index/reanalysis archives do not establish real-time historical availability.

For the response, distinguish **physical motivation**, **measurement definition**, **source availability**, and **demonstrated predictive evidence**. The frozen climate-group comparison and availability sensitivity can test the retained group; they cannot establish that every correlated Pacific index is necessary. No six-way individual-index deletion or new lag search is added by this memo. Any later claim of individual incremental value needs explicitly authorized training/development-only evidence. Correlations, learned attention, climate-relevance weights and attribution summaries do not identify causal effects. JJAS mechanisms also do not imply the same sign or strength in every calendar month or each of the five frozen study domains.
